The **Nepali Date** is based on the **Bikram Sambat** (Devanagari: **विक्रम संवत्**, abbreviated as **BS** or **B.S.**), the official national civil calendar of **Nepal**. 

Used across all organs of the Nepalese state—from parliamentary legislation and Supreme Court decrees to banking operations, taxation, school academic sessions, and day-to-day citizen records—Bikram Sambat is a sophisticated **solar sidereal calendar** with roots extending over two millennia.

---

## Historical Genesis of Bikram Sambat

### The Founding in 57 BCE
Bikram Sambat originated in **57 BCE (Before Common Era)** in the ancient city of **Ujjain** (in modern Madhya Pradesh, India). According to widespread historical tradition, the era was instituted by **King Vikramaditya of Ujjain** to commemorate his decisive military victory over the invading Saka (Indo-Scythian) forces, liberating the region and relieving the financial debt of his citizens.

Because the era was inaugurated in 57 BCE, Bikram Sambat is approximately **56.7 years ahead** of the Western Gregorian calendar (Common Era / AD).

### Evolution of the Era's Inscriptional Name
Epigraphic and numismatic records show that the name of this calendar era evolved across three distinct phases:

1. **Krita Era (कृत संवत्)**: Earliest stone inscriptions from the 3rd to 5th centuries CE referred to this system as *Krita*, possibly honoring a foundational event or sovereign leader.
2. **Malava Era (मालव संवत्)**: Between the 5th and 9th centuries CE, the era was called *Malava Gana Sambat* (the era of the Malava republic), corresponding to the migration and governance of the Malava tribe.
3. **Vikrama Samvat (विक्रम संवत्)**: By the 9th and 10th centuries CE, starting prominently around the Dholpur stone inscription (dated 842 CE / 898 BS), the era became permanently identified with King Vikramaditya.

## The Calendar Eras of Nepal

Nepal has one of the world's richest epigraphic and calendar traditions, having utilized several prominent eras throughout its documented history:

### 1. Saka Sambat (शक संवत् - 78 CE)
Introduced in ancient Nepal during the Licchavi period, the Saka Sambat was the primary calendar of early royal inscriptions. The oldest dated stone epigraph in Nepal—the pillar inscription of **King Manadeva I at the Changu Narayan Temple**—is dated **386 Saka Sambat (464 CE / 521 BS)**.

### 2. Manadeva Sambat / Amshuverma Era (576 CE)
During the late Licchavi period under rulers such as King Amshuverma and King Jisnugupta, a shortened regional calendar system known as the Manadeva Sambat was employed across the Kathmandu Valley for public administration and religious foundations.

### 3. Nepal Sambat (नेपाल संवत् - 879 CE / 936 BS)
Founded by **Shankhadhar Sakhwa** on Kartik Shukla Pratipada in 879 CE after he cleared the debts of all citizens in the Kathmandu Valley during the reign of King Raghavadeva. Nepal Sambat served as the principal civic, lunar, and commercial calendar of the Malla Dynasty (1200–1768 CE) and remains revered today as a national heritage calendar of Nepal.

### 4. Bikram Sambat in Ancient and Medieval Nepal
While Nepal Sambat predominated in the Kathmandu Valley's domestic trade, Bikram Sambat maintained continuous usage across western and northern Nepal:
- In the **Khasa Malla Empire** of the Karnali basin (centered in the Sinja Valley, Jumla), Bikram Sambat was the undisputed official calendar. The **Dullu Stone Pillar of King Prithvi Malla** (located in Dailekh) is engraved in Bikram Sambat **1414 BS (1357 CE)** using the ancient Khas language.
- King Jayasthiti Malla (reigned 1382–1395 CE) and King Pratap Malla of Kathmandu utilized Bikram Sambat alongside Nepal Sambat in stone treaties, temple edicts, and Sanskrit panegyrics.

### 5. Unification and Official Adoption in Nepal (1768–1901 CE)
When **King Prithvi Narayan Shah of Gorkha** unified Nepal and established Kathmandu as the capital in **1825 BS (1768 CE)**, he adopted Bikram Sambat as the standard calendar for royal seals (*Lal Mohar*), military directives, treaties, and administrative governance.

In **1958 BS (1901 CE)**, during the premiership of Rana Prime Minister **Chandra Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana**, Bikram Sambat was formally promulgated by imperial decree as the **sole official civil and administrative calendar of the Kingdom of Nepal**, standardizing astronomical solar calculations across all government offices.

## Astronomical Foundations & Calendar Mechanics

Unlike the Western Gregorian calendar, which is a **solar tropical** calendar based on the cycle of the seasons (equinoxes and solstices), Bikram Sambat is a **solar sidereal (*Nirayana*)** calendar governed by the principles of classical Hindu astronomy (*Surya Siddhanta*).

### 1. The Sidereal Solar Year (*Nirayana Saur Varsha*)
A sidereal year is the time it takes the Earth to complete one full revolution around the Sun relative to the fixed stars.
- The tropical year (used by Gregorian) equals approximately **365.2422 days**.
- The sidereal year (used by Bikram Sambat) equals approximately **365.25636 days**.
- Due to the precession of the equinoxes (*Ayanamsha*), the sidereal year advances forward relative to the tropical year by approximately **1 day every 71.6 years**.

### 2. Solar Ingress (*Sankranti*)
In Bikram Sambat, the first day of every month begins upon the Sun's transit (*Sankranti*) from one zodiac constellation (*Rashi*) into the next:

- **Mesha Sankranti (Aries Ingress)**: Marks the entrance of the Sun into the constellation of Mesha (Aries). This exact astronomical occurrence defines **Baisakh 1**, celebrating **Nepali New Year (*Nawa Barsha*)**.
- **Makar Sankranti (Capricorn Ingress)**: Marks the Sun's entrance into Makara, signaling the winter solstice turning point and the festival of **Maghe Sankranti**.

### 3. Kepler's Second Law & Variable Month Lengths (29 to 32 Days)
A defining characteristic of Bikram Sambat is that **month lengths vary between 29 and 32 days from year to year**.

This is not arbitrary; it is a direct consequence of celestial mechanics:
1. According to **Kepler's Second Law**, Earth travels faster along its elliptical orbit when it is closest to the Sun (perihelion, occurring in early January) and slower when it is farthest from the Sun (aphelion, occurring in early July).
2. Because the Earth moves faster during the northern hemisphere's winter, the Sun appears to traverse the winter constellations (Dhanu, Makara, Kumbha) in less time. Consequently, winter months like **Kartik, Mangsir, Poush, and Magh** typically span **29 to 30 days**.
3. During the northern hemisphere's summer, the Earth moves slower in its orbit, taking longer to traverse constellations like Mithuna and Karkata. Consequently, summer months like **Jestha, Ashadh, Shrawan, and Bhadra** typically span **31 to 32 days**.

> [!NOTE]
> Because the exact moment of Sankranti can occur at any hour of the day or night, canonical Panchanga rules determine whether the new month begins on the same solar day (if Sankranti occurs before sunset or noon) or the following solar day. This produces variations where a given month may have 31 days in one year, but 32 days in another.

## The Nepalese Fiscal Year (*Aarthik Barsha*)

In Nepal, both public finance and commercial corporate reporting run on an official fiscal cycle defined entirely by Bikram Sambat dates:

- **Fiscal Year Start**: **1 Shrawan** (approximately mid-July).
- **Fiscal Year End**: **Last day of Ashadh** (approximately mid-July of the following year).
- **Format**: Written as `YYYY/YY` in BS (for example, `2081/82 BS`).
- **Quarterly Accounting Windows**:
  - **Q1 (First Quarter)**: Shrawan 1 – Kartik end
  - **Q2 (Second Quarter)**: Mangsir 1 – Poush end
  - **Q3 (Third Quarter)**: Magh 1 – Chaitra end
  - **Q4 (Fourth Quarter)**: Baisakh 1 – Ashadh end

All corporate income tax filings, government budget announcements (constitutionally mandated on 15 Jestha BS), and audited balance sheets operate strictly within this fiscal boundary.

| **Reference Epoch** | 57 BCE (Victory of Vikramaditya) | 1 CE (Traditional Anno Domini) |
| **Year Discrepancy** | Approx **56 years, 8 months ahead** | Baseline (+0) |
| **New Year Day** | **1 Baisakh** (mid-April) | January 1 |
| **Month Calculation** | Astrological solar ingress (*Sankranti*) | Arbitrarily fixed human convention |
| **Month Lengths** | Variable dynamically: **29, 30, 31, 32 days** | Fixed: 28/29, 30, 31 days |
| **Leap Day Mechanism** | Natural solar variance across constellations | Quadrennial Leap Day (+1 day in Feb) |

| **Official Civil Status** | **National Calendar of Nepal** | International standard (ISO 8601) |

## Technical Implications for Software Engineering

Building software applications that handle Bikram Sambat presents unique architectural requirements that do not exist with purely algorithmic calendars:

### 1. Mathematical Algorithms Cannot Predict Month Lengths
In the Gregorian calendar, determining if a year is a leap year requires a simple formula:
```
(year % 4 === 0 && year % 100 !== 0) || (year % 400 === 0)
```
In Bikram Sambat, **no such modular formula exists**. Because month lengths depend on the precise astronomical minute of the Sun's transit across constellations as observed from Kathmandu, month lengths are certified yearly by the **Nepal Panchanga Nirnayak Samiti**.

### 2. High-Precision Ephemeris Lookup Tables
Reliable date libraries must embed pre-computed, verified lookup matrices containing the exact month lengths for each historical and future Bikram Sambat year.

The **Nepali Date Library** embeds validated ephemeris data covering **1976 BS through 2100 BS** (spanning more than 124 years), ensuring 100% deterministic arithmetic and conversions.

### 3. Database Architecture Recommendation
When architecting systems supporting Bikram Sambat:
- **Storage Layer**: Store all temporal values in databases as standard UTC timestamps or ISO-8601 Gregorian dates (`YYYY-MM-DD`).
- **Application Layer**: Use `nepali-date-library` to convert UTC timestamps to Bikram Sambat dates for UI display, fiscal reports, date pickers, and localized formatting.
- **Form Inputs**: Convert user-entered Bikram Sambat values to Gregorian prior to saving in persistent storage.

### 4. Nepal Standard Time (NPT)
Nepal Standard Time is **UTC+05:45**, one of the few non-hourly timezones in the world. It is calculated based on the meridian passing through the summit of **Mount Gauri Shankar** at longitude **86°15' East**. When performing date calculations near midnight, ensure that your application accounts for the +05:45 offset to avoid single-day boundary shifts.
